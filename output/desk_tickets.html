Ticket 101: Bulldog tee

Customer order from Tauhid Zaman: "Needs a tee in size S."

Type
customer_order
Item

#501 · Bulldog Print Co · $840 · due 2026-08-28 · open, 3 days overdue · "Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S"
Supplier
Bulldog Print Co (apparel reprint), lead 5 days, cannot accept new orders while #501 is unpaid
Restock cost
1 × $8.00 unit cost (list $28.00)

Expected

Business issue

A customer wants a size S tee that is out of stock. The only apparel supplier is blocked because invoice 501 is overdue, and that invoice is for a rush reprint of this exact item. The ticket can't be fulfilled until the invoice is paid and a new order is placed.

Initial assignment

Boss → Inventory

This is a stock question first: is size S on the shelf, and if not, who can supply it and when? Inventory's main tool answers all of that in one call (stock, shortfall, vendor specialty, lead time, unpaid invoices). It also surfaces invoice 501 as the blocker, which tells Inventory it needs Accounting.

Agent collaboration

  1. Boss reads the ticket and asks Inventory whether 1 × size S can ship and, if not, how to restock it.
  2. Inventory finds 0 on hand. It picks Bulldog Print Co by specialty (apparel) and sees can_accept_new_orders: false because of invoice 501. Inventory can't create orders or payments, so it asks Accounting directly: pay invoice 501, then order 1 × CC-TEE-WHITE S from vendor 1 at $8. It passes the vendor ID, SKU, size, qty and unit cost so Accounting doesn't re-check stock.
  3. Accounting checks cash, queues the $840 invoice payment, and then the $8 purchase order. The order is allowed once the invoice payment is queued, but it only executes after the invoice is actually paid. Accounting returns both approval-request IDs to Inventory.
  4. Inventory reports back to Boss: no stock, restock is queued, and expected arrival is 2026-09-05 (business date + 5 lead days), pending approval.
  5. Boss asks Customer Service to draft a reply to Tauhid Zaman. It passes the decision: size S is out of stock, a restock is being arranged, and arrival is around 5 Sep, not yet confirmed.
  6. Customer Service saves the draft. It uses the product name, not the SKU, and keeps out invoices and payment details.
  7. Boss sets the ticket to awaiting_approval and lists the two approvals in the order a human must approve them.

Not involved: Facilities (no premises issue).

2. Agent performance by ticket

Ticket 101: Bulldog tee in size S (Tauhid Zaman)

Who did what. The Boss read the ticket and handed it to Inventory with a precise brief: check stock and the vendor, coordinate invoice 501 and a PO if needed, and return the approval IDs. Inventory made a single call, check_stock_and_vendor_status("CC-TEE-WHITE","S",1). That showed 0 on hand, Bulldog Print Co as the apparel vendor, and invoice 501 ($840, 3 days overdue) blocking new orders.

Inventory can't create orders, so it asked Accounting directly. It passed the vendor ID, SKU, size, quantity and the $8 unit cost, so Accounting didn't have to look up stock again. Accounting checked get_cash_position first, then called request_payment for invoice 501 and create_purchase_order for one tee, in that order. Finally the Boss asked Customer Service for the customer reply and set the ticket to awaiting_approval.

Assessment. This was the team's best ticket. The delegation went to the right specialist first, and the Inventory → Accounting handoff is exactly what the peer-to-peer design was built for. Every MCP call was necessary; there were no repeats. The safety rules held, too. The PO was only accepted because the invoice payment was already queued, and when I clicked approvals afterwards the database refused nothing because I approved #2 before #3. The tool would have blocked the PO if I hadn't: the same request was refused three times in earlier tests, audit records #186, #238 and #295.

Weaknesses. 101 was the most expensive ticket: 37,072 input tokens and 30.7 s for what is, in the end, two database writes and a draft. The draft also slipped against my own Customer Service rules. It calls the product "Bulldog tee" instead of the catalogue name "Classic Bulldog Tee", and it tells the customer the restock depends on "required approvals", which is internal process the prompt says to leave out. The Boss's instruction to Customer Service explicitly said "invoice 501 payment and restock/PO are pending human approval", so the leak started with the Boss's wording, not with Customer Service. Finally, the draft is now out of date: after I approved #2 and #3, PO #1 is placed with an ETA of 2026-09-05, but the draft still describes the restock as conditional. Nothing in the system rewrites a draft after approval.

Ticket 102: Rent due (Elm City Properties)

Facilities verified the notice with get_lease_payment_status(1): landlord Elm City Properties, $2,400, due 2026-09-02, 2 days after the business date, and no rent payment recorded. It then asked Accounting to queue the rent, passing the amount, due date and ticket. Accounting checked cash and queued request #1. Facilities saved the landlord draft ("a payment request is being processed, pending human approval. It has not yet been paid"), and the Boss set the ticket to awaiting_approval.

Assessment. Correct, quick (7.3 s) and safe. The order was right: verify against the lease first, then pay, and the draft didn't claim the rent was paid. Running 102 first was also the right priority, because rent due within 7 days sits at the top of our payment policy. After I approved, leases.next_due rolled forward to 2026-10-02 and payment #1 was recorded.

Wasted steps. Facilities made two calls it didn't need. It called get_ticket(102) even though the orchestrator had already given it the ticket brief, and get_business_date() even though get_lease_payment_status already returns the business date and days_until_due. That's 8 MCP calls where 6 would do. It didn't change the outcome, but it shows agents being cautious and re-checking facts they already had.

Ticket 103: Bulk hoodie discount (Yale AI Club)

Who did what. The Boss sent the price override to Accounting, with the note that a bulk order counts by the quantity requested. In one step, Accounting asked Inventory about supply, checked get_cash_position and ran evaluate_bulk_discount("CC-HOOD-NAVY","M",20,15). That gave $49.30 per unit, a 55.4% margin and a 12-unit shortfall. Inventory confirmed 8 in size M and reported that restocking 12 would cost $264. Accounting then queued request_discount_approval for the 8 units it could actually supply and deliberately did not try a PO, because only $152 was uncommitted. The Boss asked Customer Service for the reply and noted for the human that $112 more cash would be needed before the remaining 12 could be ordered.

Assessment. The core business judgement was right, and I think it was the hardest call of the three. The agents split the request into the part the shop could do (8 hoodies at a policy-compliant discount) and the part it couldn't fund ($264 against $152). They also correctly treated the discount as a decision that moves no cash. The $112 figure is correct arithmetic ($264 − $152).

Weakness. The customer draft promises more than the decision supports: "If a restock is approved, the estimated arrival is September 5, 2026". No restock was queued, so that date isn't backed by any request in the system. The audit trail shows where it came from: the Boss's own instruction to Customer Service included "if a PO is later approved, estimated delivery is 2026-09-05". So Customer Service did what it was told, and the problem was the Boss's framing. Customer Service did keep out the internal details the Boss passed along (the $264 cost and the $152 cash), which is what its prompt asks for.

Run-to-run variation. This ticket was the least stable across runs. In the second development run (audit trail, 2026-10-15 00:20:54) the Boss closed 103 as resolved with no discount, because it read "8 on hand" as failing the 10-unit bulk threshold. That came from ambiguous wording in our policy, which I then fixed. All four later runs of 103 produced the same correct outcome as the final run.

What the differences tell me. All three predictions of who the Boss would contact first were right, and so was the set of agents involved on every ticket. 4. Would a single agent have been simpler?

Where one agent would clearly have been better: ticket 102. The work was one lease lookup, one cash check, one payment request, one draft and one status update. A single agent with all those MCP tools could do it in about five tool calls and a handful of model requests. Our team used 11 model requests, 8 tool calls and two handoffs (Boss → Facilities → Accounting) to reach the same result. A big part of the 27,736 input tokens is overhead from the design: each time a specialist starts, it receives its full role prompt plus the shared policy file, so the same policy text is sent several times per ticket. The two redundant calls Facilities made (re-fetching the ticket and the business date) also suggest that agents who only see part of the picture re-check things to be safe.

The same applies to other plain lookups and updates: checking stock for a size that is on hand, reading a lease, or setting a ticket status. None of these needs coordination between departments, and a single agent would mean fewer handoffs, lower latency (102 still took 7 s; 101 and 103 took 30–35 s), fewer tokens, and one conversation to read when something goes wrong instead of a chain of nested delegations.

Where the specialists earned their keep: tickets 101 and 103. In 101, the stock problem turned out to be an accounting problem (an unpaid vendor invoice), and in 103 a pricing question turned out to be an inventory and cash problem. The specialist split helped in three concrete ways:

  • Focused tool access acted as a guard rail. Inventory has no payment tools, so on 101 it had to hand the money decisions to Accounting, which checked cash before queuing anything. Only the Boss could change ticket status. A single agent holding all 14 agent-usable tools would rely only on its prompt to keep those boundaries.
  • Focused prompts carried focused policy. Accounting's prompt carries the payment priority order and the discount rule. On 103 that's why it queued the discount only for the 8 units in stock and didn't attempt the $264 PO, without the Boss telling it to.
  • The handoffs are visible. Because work passed between named agents, the audit trail shows who decided what. For 103 I could trace the September 5 date to the Boss's instruction instead of blaming Customer Service. In a single-agent transcript that would have been one long chain of reasoning.

My conclusion is that the best design is a hybrid. Route simple, single-department tickets like 102 to one capable agent (or one specialist with no Boss loop), and bring in the full team only when a ticket crosses departments, as 101 and 103 did.

5. Three more problems the current system can already solve

One limitation applies to all three: the system has no tool or endpoint for creating a ticket, so each scenario starts with someone adding a row to the tickets table. Everything after that uses agents and MCP tools that already exist.

5.1 Restock the Crest Mug from Elm City Gifts

Problem. CC-MUG-CREST (size OS) has been at 0 on hand the whole time, but no ticket asked about it. Suppose a customer or staff member raises one.

Agents and tools. Boss → Inventory: check_stock_and_vendor_status("CC-MUG-CREST","OS",n) shows 0 on hand. The supplier would be Elm City Gifts (vendor 2, "mugs and small goods", 3-day lead time, no unpaid invoices). Inventory → Accounting: get_cash_position, then create_purchase_order(vendor 2, …) priced at the $3.50 unit cost. Boss → Customer Service: save_message_draft. Boss: update_ticket_status("awaiting_approval").

Outcome. A small PO queued for human approval with an ETA of business date + 3 days. With $152 left, about 24 mugs ($84) is affordable, and the tool would refuse anything over $152. Approval executes it and the ticket resolves.

Why it works today. It's the same path as ticket 101, minus the invoice blocker. Every table and tool involved (vendors, pricing, purchase_orders, approval_requests) already exists.

5.2 A customer order that can ship from stock

Problem. A customer asks for a Classic Bulldog Tee in size M (5 on hand) or a Basic Hoodie Big Yale in size L (14 on hand).

Agents and tools. Boss → Inventory (check_stock_and_vendor_status returns in_stock: true, shortfall 0) → Customer Service (save_message_draft confirming availability) → Boss (update_ticket_status("resolved")).

Outcome. Resolved in one pass with no approval, because no money moves. The update_ticket_status tool lets a ticket close as resolved only when no approvals are pending, so this path is allowed.

Why it works today. Inventory's main tool already reports in_stock and the shortfall, and the Boss prompt allows resolved when nothing needs approval. Of the six cases in sections 5 and 6, this is the one where a single agent would be the obvious choice.

5.3 The October rent notice, when cash can't cover it

Problem. After our run, lease 1's next_due is 2026-10-02 and checking holds only $152. Suppose Elm City Properties sends the next rent notice.

Agents and tools. Boss → Facilities: get_lease_payment_status(1) shows $2,400 due in 32 days, one rent payment already recorded, and can_cover_rent: false. Facilities → Accounting: request_payment("rent", 1, …) is refused by the tool, because $2,400 exceeds the $152 that isn't already committed and no incoming cash is assumed. Facilities drafts a landlord reply; the Boss marks the ticket blocked and explains the shortfall to the human.

Outcome. No payment, a clear explanation, and an escalation to the human. That's the correct business answer given the data.

Why it works today. The cash-protection rule is enforced inside request_payment, not only in the prompts, and blocked is a valid ticket status. This scenario tests the system's safety path the way 101–103 tested its normal path.

6. Three problems that need new capabilities

6.1 Actually working with suppliers: sending the PO and receiving the stock

Problem. After ticket 101, PO #1 is placed with an ETA of 2026-09-05. But nothing told Bulldog Print Co about it, and when the tees arrive nothing will add them to stock: inventory for CC-TEE-WHITE size S still says 0. Tauhid's order can't actually be completed by the system.

Current limitation. We deliberately have no external communication, and there's no "goods received" tool. A PO's lifecycle ends at placed, and no tool ever increases inventory.qty.

What's needed.

  • A send_purchase_order tool connected to the supplier's email or order system. It would stay behind human approval, like payments.
  • A receive_purchase_order(po_id, qty_received) tool that adds stock to inventory, closes the PO and flags short deliveries.
  • A get_open_purchase_orders tool, so late deliveries can be chased.

New agent? Not at first. Inventory can own receiving. A separate Procurement agent would only be worth it once supplier communications (quotes, confirmations, delays) become frequent.

How it would work. After approval, Accounting releases the PO through send_purchase_order. When stock arrives, Inventory records it, and the Boss reopens the linked ticket so Customer Service can tell Tauhid the tee is ready.

6.2 Really talking to customers, and taking requests in

Problem. All three replies (drafts #1–#3) are still sitting in message_drafts with status draft, and the database has no email address or phone number for Tauhid Zaman, Elm City Properties or Yale AI Club. Requests also only reach the agents if someone inserts a ticket row by hand.

Current limitation. The no-external-communication rule is a design choice, but it means the shop can't close the loop with a customer. There's no customers table, no send tool, and no create_ticket tool or endpoint. The drafts also go stale: draft #2 still says the tee restock is conditional even though the PO is now placed.

What's needed.

  • A customers table with contact details.
  • A create_ticket tool behind an inbound email or web-form integration.
  • A send_message(draft_id, approved_by) tool that sends only drafts a human has approved, using the same pattern as execute_approved_payment.
  • A step that regenerates drafts after approvals change the facts.

New agent? No. Customer Service already owns this role; it needs the tools. An intake step (classifying incoming email into ticket types) could be a small new agent, or part of the Boss's triage.

How it would work. An email from the Yale AI Club becomes ticket 104 automatically. After the run, the operator reviews and approves the draft on the dashboard, and the message is sent and logged in the audit trail.

6.3 Demand forecasting and proactive restocking

Problem. The team only reacts to tickets. Nobody noticed that the Crest Mug sits at 0, or that the Yale AI Club's 20-hoodie request means size M (8 left) will run out at the start of term. A real shop would want restocks planned before customers ask.

Current limitation. The database has no sales history at all: only current stock, prices and open obligations. Agents only run when a ticket is started, so there's no scheduled scan. Cash is outflow-only, so the system also can't project whether future sales would fund the 12 extra hoodies.

What's needed.

  • A sales table, plus tools like record_sale, get_sales_history(sku, days) and list_low_stock(threshold).
  • A forecasting tool (moving average or seasonal model).
  • A scheduler that runs a check every morning.
  • Allowing expected incoming cash in Accounting's planning, which would mean changing the outflow-only rule we used for this simulation.

New agent? Yes, a Planning agent makes sense here. It's a different kind of job (looking ahead, statistical, not tied to any ticket), and keeping it separate stops it mixing with the ticket workflow. It would hand restock recommendations to Inventory and Accounting through the existing ask_agent mechanism.

How it would work. Each morning the Planning agent flags SKUs where forecast demand exceeds stock plus open POs, ranks them by margin and urgency, and opens restock tickets. Those then follow the same approval path as ticket 101.

7. Overall system design

Strengths of the five-agent architecture. On these three tickets the architecture did what I designed it for. The Boss routed every ticket to the right first specialist. Specialists collaborated without the Boss relaying everything: Facilities → Accounting on 102, Inventory → Accounting on 101, Accounting → Inventory on 103. And each agent stayed inside its permissions. In 54 delegations across every run in the audit trail, no agent ever tried a human-only tool. Each agent's prompt is also short and specific, which made it easy to fix behaviour locally. When 103 misread the bulk rule in an early run, I changed one sentence in shop_policies.md; I didn't need to rework anything else.

Weaknesses. Coordination costs tokens and time: about 97k input tokens and 73 s for three small tickets. Errors also carry through handoffs, as the September 5 promise on 103 showed. The safety guards I built for runaway delegation (cycle detection, a depth limit of 3, at most 8 delegations, the repeat-request cache) never actually triggered: 0 refused or cached delegations in the whole audit trail. That's good news, but it means those protections are tested only by design, not by real use.

MCP as the shared interface. MCP was the best decision in the project. Business rules live in the tools, not just in prompts, and that's what made the system trustworthy. The vendor-invoice rule, the no-negative-balance rule and the no-duplicate-payment rule held regardless of what any model said: the "PO before invoice" approval was refused by the tool three times in testing (records #186, #238, #295), and a second approval of the same request was refused (#189). The same tools were reused by the agents, the FastAPI backend, the approval desk and Claude Code itself in Problem 4, with no second database layer anywhere.

Specialisation vs. coordination overhead. 101 and 103 justified their handoffs; 102 didn't. The real cost is less the number of agents than the repeated context: each agent start re-sends the policy file. Passing the ticket brief along with every delegation was a cheap improvement. The first 103 run used 47,172 input tokens and the final one 32,006, though some of that is also run-to-run variation.

Human approval for money. Human approval was essential, and the run showed why the rules need to be in code. The agents queued $3,248 of payments, and every one stayed pending until an approval. The PO for 101 couldn't run until invoice 501 was paid; the $264 restock never existed because Accounting couldn't queue what cash didn't cover. The run also exposed a weakness I have to be honest about: in Problem 9 the approve clicks were made by Claude Code at my instruction, recorded as "Michael Zheng (via Claude Code)". The operator name on the dashboard is free text with no login, so "human approval" is currently enforced by convention, not authentication.

The audit trail. The audit trail is what made this reflection possible. It records every tool call, delegation, decision and approval with timestamps, inputs and outputs. That let me count redundant calls on 102, trace the September 5 date on 103 back to the Boss, measure each ticket's tokens, and confirm the Cash tab against real payment IDs. It's append-only (366 records, nothing ever overwritten), so the failed early run of 103 is still there as evidence instead of having been quietly replaced.

What I would improve next:

  1. Route by complexity. Send single-department tickets like 102 to one agent and keep the team for tickets like 101 and 103, which cross departments.
  2. Tighten Customer Service. Give it a product-name lookup and an automatic check that removes internal words like "approval" or "invoice" from drafts. Have the Boss pass decisions, not speculative dates.
  3. Regenerate drafts after approvals, so a message like draft #2 reflects the placed PO before a human sends it.
  4. Real operator authentication on the approval endpoint, so approved_by is a verified identity.
  5. Per-agent usage in the audit trail. Today tokens are only known per ticket, so I can't say how much of 101's 37k tokens belongs to Inventory versus Accounting.
  6. A repeatable evaluation set. Run 101–103 several times and check the outcome, approvals and drafts automatically, so failures like the early 103 run are caught as regressions.
  7. The receiving, sending and intake tools from section 6, so a ticket like 101 ends with the tee actually reaching Tauhid.

8. Evidence gaps and open issues

  • No single-agent baseline was run. The comparison in section 4 is reasoned from our measured multi-agent numbers; I didn't measure a single-agent version of the same tickets.
  • Token use is recorded per ticket, not per agent, so I can't split cost between agents on the same ticket.
  • The approvals were clicked by Claude Code on my instruction, not by me personally. They're recorded under the label "Michael Zheng (via Claude Code)".
  • The delegation limits were never triggered in any real run, so their effectiveness rests on the design and code, not on observed events.
  • Run times vary a lot with the model gateway. Ticket 102 took 43.0 s in its first development run and 7.3 s in the final run, with the same steps. The timings above are from a single run.
  • Open business issues in the data: the drafts are unsent, and two of them (#2 and #3) need editing before sending. The 12 extra hoodies for Yale AI Club remain unfunded ($112 short). The tee is on order but not yet received into inventory.

9. Follow-up: what I fixed after writing this

After this reflection I fixed the defects it identified. The sections above still describe the Problem 9 run as it happened.

  • Customer drafts are checked in code. The draft tool now refuses customer drafts with internal terms, SKU codes or a missing catalogue name. The ticket brief carries the product name, and the Boss passes only customer-safe facts.
  • Delivery dates need a real order. Agents may only quote a date for items with a queued purchase order (the 103 issue).
  • Fewer redundant lookups. Specialists lost the redundant ticket and date lookups (the 102 issue).
  • Stale drafts are flagged. Deciding an approval now marks that ticket's drafts "needs review" (the 101 issue).
  • Per-agent tokens are recorded on every delegation.

A re-run on a scratch database, with the same order and approvals, used 6 MCP calls per ticket (down from 8, 7 and 7), produced clean drafts with no retries, and again ended at $152. Total tokens did not fall: most input tokens are prompts re-sent to each agent. So the single-agent routing idea in section 7 is still the main lever for cost. Approval login is still open. Full details are in output/harness.md, section 13.
